Wave speed is the propagation speed along the string, not the up-and-down speed of a bead. Greater tension T at the same linear mass density μ makes waves faster: c = √(T/μ). Changing it while moving can change the stored energy. Damping dissipates mechanical energy, making motion fade.

What’s real here?

A linear transverse-wave model of a uniform string: 129 points, each tugged by its neighbors. “Transverse” means the material moves perpendicular to the wave’s direction of travel. The screen exaggerates displacement so you can see it; the model assumes small slopes and constant tension.

Boundary conditions determine how the ends respond. Fixed ends impose zero displacement. Ends free to slide impose zero slope while the string remains under tension. A fixed end reverses a reflected pulse’s sign (phase inversion); a sliding end does not. In “Disappear,” destructive interference cancels displacement briefly, not energy.

Kinetic energy is energy of motion. Potential energy is stored by the string’s deflection under tension. The bar shows their shares, not the total energy. Damping dissipates energy; your touch can add or remove it. Trails show earlier shapes of this one string, not extra strings.

Normalized wave model: ∂²y/∂t² = c² ∂²y/∂x² − γ ∂y/∂t. Here y is transverse displacement, c is wave speed, and γ is the velocity-damping coefficient. This is not a large-deformation elastic rope with gravity. Simulation slows on overloaded devices rather than skipping unstable physics steps.
